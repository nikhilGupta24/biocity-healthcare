What Is Vitamin D and Why Does It Matter?

Vitamin D is not just a vitamin — it's a hormone precursor that your body synthesises when skin is exposed to sunlight. It plays a critical role in calcium absorption, bone mineralisation, immune function, and mood regulation. Without adequate Vitamin D, your body simply cannot absorb calcium properly, no matter how much dairy you consume.

There are two primary forms: Vitamin D2 (ergocalciferol), found in some plant foods and fungi, and Vitamin D3 (cholecalciferol), produced by your skin and found in animal-based foods. D3 is more effective at raising and maintaining blood levels.

Key Fact

Vitamin D receptors are found in almost every cell in your body — from brain cells to immune cells to heart muscle. A deficiency doesn't just affect your bones; it impacts nearly every system.

The Scale of the Problem in India

Despite being a tropical country with abundant sunlight, India faces one of the world's worst Vitamin D deficiency crises. Studies published in the Indian Journal of Endocrinology and Metabolism reveal alarming numbers across every demographic.

Symptoms You Shouldn't Ignore

Vitamin D deficiency is called a "silent epidemic" because symptoms develop gradually and are often mistaken for stress, ageing, or general fatigue. Here are the warning signs:

  • Persistent fatigue and tiredness — even with adequate sleep, you feel drained by afternoon
  • Bone pain and lower back pain — dull, aching pain that worsens with activity
  • Muscle weakness — difficulty climbing stairs, getting up from a chair, or carrying groceries
  • Frequent infections — catching colds, flu, and respiratory infections more than 3–4 times a year
  • Depression and mood changes — Vitamin D directly influences serotonin production in the brain
  • Slow wound healing — cuts and surgical wounds take noticeably longer to close
  • Hair loss — especially diffuse thinning not linked to thyroid or iron issues
  • Bone density loss — leading to osteopenia and eventually osteoporosis, particularly in women post-menopause

Warning

In children, severe Vitamin D deficiency causes rickets — softening and weakening of bones leading to bowed legs and skeletal deformities. In adults, it causes osteomalacia (bone softening) that mimics arthritis pain.

Why Are So Many Indians Deficient?

The paradox of widespread deficiency in a sun-drenched country has several explanations:

  1. Indoor lifestyle — Office workers, students, and work-from-home professionals spend 90% of daylight hours indoors. Air-conditioned buildings block UV-B rays completely.
  2. Darker skin pigmentation — Melanin acts as a natural sunscreen. People with darker skin need 3–5x more sun exposure to produce the same amount of Vitamin D as lighter-skinned individuals.
  3. Air pollution — Particulate matter in cities like Delhi, Mumbai, and Kolkata absorbs UV-B radiation before it reaches your skin. Studies show pollution can reduce Vitamin D synthesis by up to 50%.
  4. Sunscreen overuse — SPF 30 blocks 97% of UV-B rays. While sun protection is important, it virtually eliminates Vitamin D production.
  5. Dietary gaps — The Indian vegetarian diet is naturally low in Vitamin D. Egg yolks, fatty fish, and fortified dairy are the main food sources, and many Indians consume insufficient quantities.
  6. Obesity — Vitamin D is fat-soluble. In obese individuals, it gets sequestered in fat tissue, reducing bioavailability by up to 55%.

How to Get Tested: The 25-OH Vitamin D Test

The gold standard test is the 25-hydroxy Vitamin D test (also written as 25(OH)D). This blood test measures both D2 and D3 forms circulating in your blood and gives the most accurate picture of your Vitamin D status.

Treatment and Recovery Timeline

Treatment depends on the severity of deficiency. Here's what doctors typically prescribe:

Mild Deficiency (20–29 ng/mL)

Daily oral supplementation of 1,000–2,000 IU of Vitamin D3, combined with 15–20 minutes of morning sunlight exposure. Retest after 3 months.

Moderate Deficiency (10–20 ng/mL)

Weekly sachets of 60,000 IU Vitamin D3 for 8 weeks (loading phase), followed by monthly maintenance doses. Calcium supplementation (500–1,000 mg/day) is often co-prescribed to prevent hypocalcaemia during rapid repletion.

Severe Deficiency (<10 ng/mL)

Intramuscular injection of 300,000–600,000 IU as a single dose or 60,000 IU weekly for 12 weeks under close medical supervision. Calcium, magnesium, and Vitamin K2 supplementation are essential.

Important

Never self-prescribe high-dose Vitamin D supplements. Excessive Vitamin D can cause hypercalcaemia — dangerous calcium buildup that damages kidneys and heart. Always get tested first, then follow your doctor's dosing protocol.

Prevention: A Daily Protocol

Once your levels are corrected, maintaining sufficient Vitamin D requires a multi-pronged approach:

  • Sunlight first — 15–20 minutes of direct midday sun (10 AM – 2 PM) on arms and face, without sunscreen, 3–4 times per week
  • Dietary sources — fatty fish (salmon, sardines, mackerel), egg yolks, fortified milk, mushrooms exposed to UV light
  • Daily supplement — 1,000 IU Vitamin D3 as maintenance, especially during winter and monsoon months
  • Pair with Vitamin K2 — K2 directs calcium to bones rather than arteries, enhancing Vitamin D's benefits
  • Annual testing — get your 25-OH Vitamin D levels checked every 12 months to catch any decline early

For those at higher risk — elderly, pregnant women, people with dark skin, obese individuals, and those on medications like anticonvulsants or glucocorticoids — testing every 6 months is recommended.
